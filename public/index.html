    How a job runs

    Your procedures first. Paperwork every time.

    1. Book with the aircraft and the time

      Tell us the type, airport, FBO or hangar and when the crew shows. You get a price before we roll.

    2. Walkaround before we touch it

      Photos and a condition record with your crew, and a count of the port covers your maintenance team installed.

    3. Work under your rules

      Operator procedures, FBO ramp rules and airport washing rules come first. Products made for aircraft, used as labeled.

    4. Photo report within two hours

      Before and after from the same angles, what we did, what we used, and anything we noticed and reported to the crew.

    What we never do

    • Put water, cleaner or tape on pitot tubes, static ports or angle-of-attack vanes
    • Use hoses or pressure washers on the ramp
    • Use ammonia on windows, windshields or de-ice boots
    • Touch anything that counts as maintenance
    • Enter the flight deck without the crew's OK
    • Post an aircraft, cabin or tail number without written permission

    The standard

    Written down, so you don't have to take our word for it.

    Every horror story in this trade is about an opening, a chemical or a rag — almost never about a dull finish. This is what we do about each of them, on every aircraft, every time. Keep a copy as a PDF, with the forms we use, for your vendor file.

    1. Openings stay closed

      Nothing touches pitot tubes, static ports, AOA vanes, TAT probes, static wicks or drain masts. Covers and plugs are installed and removed by your maintenance staff; we count them in and out with your crew and photograph them at handback.

      Tape left on static ports has brought down an airliner. A cover count on paper is how it never happens here.

    2. No water where water shouldn't go

      Exteriors are waterless. No hoses, no pressure washers, nothing sprayed at wheel bearings, windows or the static system. Wet washing only in the airport's designated wash area, arranged in advance.

      A teaspoon of wash water in a static line has ended a flight. It also keeps us inside HCAA, PIE and Florida DEP stormwater rules.

    3. Acrylic gets the acrylic protocol

      Ammonia-free window cleaner in the SAE AMS 1535 class, flooded then wiped, straight strokes, cotton or lint-free cloth. No paper, no solvents, no dry wiping, no brushes. Windshields only as your maintenance manual allows.

      Glass cleaner crazes acrylic in weeks. It's cheaper to replace a window than to save one.

    4. Chemistry named, not just "aviation grade"

      Products whose manufacturers state they meet Boeing D6-17487 for exteriors and D6-7127 for interiors, and the relevant SAE AMS specifications, used as labeled. Your maintenance manual and approved-materials list win every argument. No wipes on leather, screens or avionics.

      The FAA does not approve cleaning products. Anyone who tells you theirs are "FAA-approved" has already told you something untrue.

    5. Towels in, towels out

      Every towel, pad and tool is counted onto the job card and counted off it. A FOD walk of the ramp footprint before we leave. Inlet and exhaust covers stay exactly as your crew set them.

      A rag in an inlet is a FOD event with a name on it.

    6. No machine near a probe, a window or a rivet line

      Test spot first. Daylight inspection photos after. Aircraft paint is thin at the rivets and usually has no clear coat, so anything that can burn through never gets near an edge.

      A burn-through is a repaint, not a touch-up.

    7. Your ramp, your rules

      FBO ramp rules and airport regulations first. Escorted or badged as the field requires. We never solicit on your ramp, and your customer stays your customer.

      FBO managers have to answer for everyone they let on the ramp. We make that an easy answer.

    8. The same people

      The owner leads the first job on every new account. After that you will know the name of everyone who touches the aircraft, and it will be the same names next month.

      Every chief pilot who praises a detailer praises the crew that shows up. Nobody praises a revolving door.

    9. It's written down

      Walkaround photos with your crew before we open a bottle. A photo report within two hours after: before and after from the same angles, products used, cover and towel counts, anything we noticed and reported. Nothing goes in a logbook. That is yours.

      You shouldn't have to walk out to the ramp to find out whether the job got done.

      Questions operators ask

      Before you hand us the keys.

      Are you insured, and for how much?

      Aviation general liability and hangarkeepers coverage is being placed through an aviation-specialist broker, and the limits will be printed here the day the policy is bound. Before our first job on your ramp you will have a certificate naming your FBO, airport and flight department as additional insured, with a waiver of subrogation. If a limit isn't printed on this page, we haven't bound it yet, and we will say so.

      Are you FAA-certified?

      Nobody is. The FAA does not certify aircraft detailers and does not approve cleaning products, and any company that says otherwise has told you something untrue on its first page. What actually exists is the manufacturers' material specifications (Boeing D6-17487 and D6-7127, the SAE AMS series), your aircraft's maintenance manual, insurance with the right endorsements, and a written procedure. We work to all four, and the procedure is published above.

      Do you wash with water?

      Almost never. Our standard exterior is a waterless dry wash with aircraft-spec wash-wax, which keeps us inside HCAA, PIE and Florida DEP rules on ramp runoff. Wet washing only happens in the airport's designated wash area, arranged in advance, with phosphate-free aviation detergents.

      Do you touch anything maintenance-related?

      No. Your maintenance team installs and removes port covers and does any post-wash checks and lubrication. We count the covers in and out with your crew, photograph them at handback, stay clear of every probe and port, and never make a logbook entry.

      Do you do ceramic coatings?

      Not as a headline, and never with a years-long promise. Aircraft paint is thin, usually has no clear coat, and takes a beating at altitude, so a coating's real life is measured in flight hours, not years. We offer paint gloss enhancement and coatings on request after the surface has been assessed in daylight, and we will tell you what to expect in hours.

      How fast can you get here?

      Members get a four-hour response and their dates held. For everyone else we answer within two hours, six in the morning to ten at night, and tell you honestly what we can do. Same day and AOG carry a flat $3,000; under 24 hours, $1,500. Both are printed on the price list, so there is never a number invented on the phone.

      Which airports do you work at?

      Thirteen around Tampa Bay. Albert Whitted (SPG) is our home field; St. Pete–Clearwater (PIE), Clearwater Executive (CLW), Tampa International (TPA), Peter O. Knight (TPF) and Tampa Executive (VDF) have travel included; Sarasota Bradenton (SRQ), Venice, Lakeland Linder (LAL), Bartow, Brooksville (BKV), Plant City and Zephyrhills are a flat $95. At each we work under the airport's written authorization or an FBO or tenant arrangement, and we tell you which applies to your field before we book, not after. The rules at each airport.

      Why are you more expensive than the other quote?

      Because of the standard above. Cover counts, towel counts, walkaround photos, a report within two hours, the same crew every time and the owner on the first job all take time that a quote-by-phone detailer isn't spending. We don't discount, and we don't quote a range and settle it on the invoice.

      Will you post photos of our aircraft?

      Never without written permission. Tail numbers, cabins, crews and customer names stay private by default, and the photo report goes to the contact you name and nobody else.

      What if something gets damaged?

      We photograph the aircraft with your crew before we start, so every existing mark is on record. If anything happens on our watch, you hear it from us on the ramp, within fifteen minutes, before you find it yourself. Then the insurance does what it is for.
